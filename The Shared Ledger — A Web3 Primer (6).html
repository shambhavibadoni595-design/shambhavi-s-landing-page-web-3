What a blockchain actually is

Strip away the hype and a is a record book copied across thousands of computers instead of locked in one office. Each new page — a — lists a batch of transactions and is sealed with a : a short fingerprint generated from everything on that page.

Here's the clever part: each block's hash also depends on the hash of the block before it. Change one word on an old page and its fingerprint changes, which breaks every fingerprint after it. That's what "immutable" really means here — not impossible to alter, just instantly obvious and expensive to fake.

Decentralization: who keeps the copy

A normal ledger has one keeper. If the bank makes an error, or the platform shuts down, the record goes with it. A blockchain hands a copy to every in the network and settles disagreements through — rules like Proof of Work or Proof of Stake that decide whose version of the next block gets accepted by the rest.

003

Cryptocurrency: entries, not coins

A coin like Bitcoin or Ether is really a line in the shared ledger saying "this much value moved from address A to address B." Your doesn't hold coins the way a leather wallet holds cash — it holds a , a secret signature proving the entries belong to you.

Lose the key and you lose access, permanently. There is no branch to walk into and no password-reset email — which is exactly the trade-off decentralization makes.

⊞

Fungible

Any one unit is interchangeable with any other, like a rupee or a dollar.

⧉

Divisible

Entries can be split into tiny fractions — you are never forced to move a whole coin.

⌁

Volatile

Prices are set by open markets and can swing sharply. This primer is not financial advice.

004
chapter

Smart contracts: rules that run themselves

A writes "if this, then that" directly into the ledger. If address A sends 1 ETH, release the ticket. If the auction closes, send the funds to the highest bidder. Once deployed, nobody can pull it aside for a favor — it executes exactly as written, every time.

It resembles a vending machine more than a legal contract: no clerk, no landlord, no discretion. That reliability is the entire pitch, and also the entire risk — a bug written into the machine runs just as faithfully as a correct one.

input arrives→ rule is checked→ outcome executes→ result recorded

No step in that row waits for a human to approve it. That is the whole idea — and the reason auditing the code matters so much.

005
chapter

NFTs: unique entries in a fungible world

One Bitcoin is worth exactly one Bitcoin, always — that's what "fungible" means. An (non-fungible token) is a ledger entry that instead says: this one thing is unique, and this address owns it — closer to a deed or a ticket stub than to money.

What's provably owned is often just the deed, not the file itself — the artwork usually lives elsewhere, with the token pointing to it.

DAOs: bylaws written as code

A — Decentralized Autonomous Organization — runs a group the way a smart contract runs a transaction: budgets and voting rules live in code, not a binder in a filing cabinet. Members hold tokens that double as voting weight, and proposals pass or fail by the ledger's own tally.

No CEO signs off. The organization's "decision" is whatever the code and the vote produce — for better and for worse.

Why a transaction sometimes costs money

Every computer in the network has to redo your work to agree with you. is the fee that pays for that effort — and it rises when everyone wants in at once.
